# Abstract

Requirements models of business systems describe entities whose rows are created, linked by
foreign keys, moved through lifecycles and deleted. Checking fair liveness on such models, such
as "every paid order is eventually shipped, provided that no order's shipping step is starved",
by explicit-state search runs into two kinds of state explosion. The first comes from
sub-lifecycles that a property observes only through a few values. The second comes from
interchangeable rows. The usual remedies are not sound here. A quotient by divergence-blind
stuttering bisimulation is unsound for weak fairness of the next-state relation. Symmetry
reduction in TLC is documented as unsound for liveness. Symmetry over all row slots does not
commute with creation in the lowest free slot. We present two reductions for this setting. The
*sub-lifecycle quotient* replaces an independent sub-lifecycle by the blocks of a stable
partition, and marks blocks that can loop silently so that the quotient stays sound under
`WF(Next)`. *Instance symmetry* uses a state-dependent group that permutes only the slots that are
not absent and relabels foreign keys, and it carries fair instances along each quotient edge's
permutation. We prove both sound for per-instance weak fairness over a small calculus, and we
mechanize their flat cases in Lean 4. The implementation does not trust the code that computes
either reduction. Every run checks the hypotheses that depend on computed data and falls back to a
sound alternative when a check fails, and every printed counterexample is replayed on the unreduced
system before it prints. On the four largest of nine models, `verify` with the quotient finishes in
4 to 20 s. Without it, it does not finish within 300 s. BFS on the whole model, with no cone,
passes 10 million states on every domain model without finishing.

# 1 Introduction

Lifecycle models are a common way to state business requirements. An *order* is placed, paid,
shipped and closed. A *payment* belongs to an order through a foreign key. A *shop* is opened,
inspected and suspended. rdra-ish is a requirements DSL of this kind. It is a textual form of the
RDRA requirements method, with entities, use cases that create, update, link and delete rows,
guards written as conditions, and rules and temporal properties over rows. A model `M` at a bound
`n` (slots per entity) and an integer range denotes a finite transition system `T_n(M)`. The tool
decides state rules, `always`, `stable`, `precedes`, deadlocks, `can_reach`, and the liveness
forms `eventually` and `leads_to` under per-instance weak fairness. It does this with an
explicit-state checker of its own, and can run TLC on the same printed system for comparison.

Liveness is where the state space hurts. A safety check can be decided on a small *cone of
influence*: the variables that the check reads and those that can affect them. A liveness
property under weak fairness needs a whole graph to search for fair cycles, and two features of
lifecycle models make that graph large.

- **Observed sub-lifecycles.** An order's shipping step is guarded by "the shop is open". The
  shop has a lifecycle of its own, with inspections, suspensions and staff changes, of which the
  order observes one bit. The cone of the property contains the whole shop lifecycle.
- **Interchangeable rows.** Two orders in the same state are interchangeable, except where the
  model can tell them apart: by creation order, by a foreign key, or by a comparison of ids.

Both have classic answers that fail in this setting, in ways that the tool's adversarial test
corpus records.

*Stuttering quotients and `WF(Next)`.* Merging the shop's states that look the same to the order
is a stuttering bisimulation quotient. It preserves CTL\* without next. TLA+ conventionally
conjoins weak fairness of the whole next-state relation, `WF(Next)`, which says that a behaviour
does not stop while some step is enabled. Such a specification is not stuttering invariant at the
level of a quotient. A behaviour that loops forever inside one block, such as a latch set and
cleared while a gate stays shut, satisfies `WF(Next)` and starves the order. Its image in the
quotient *stops* in a block that has a visible way out, which `WF(Next)` forbids. A checker that
searches the quotient answers `ok`, and that answer is wrong (Section 2).

*Symmetry and creation.* Symmetry reduction permutes the slots of an entity. In rdra-ish, as in
TLA+ specifications that allocate the first free slot, creation takes the *lowest absent* slot.
A permutation that moves an absent slot does not commute with creation. In the corpus case
`presence_first_absent_asymmetry`, only the second order can ever be a rush order, so a checker
that decides one binding and assumes the others alike misses a liveness violation (Section 2). TLC's symmetry
sets have a second problem: TLC's documentation warns that liveness checking under symmetry can
miss violations or report spurious ones.

**Contributions.**

1. *A sub-lifecycle quotient for fair liveness* (Section 4). A cone is split into a part `T`,
   kept concrete, and a sub-lifecycle `S` that `T` reads only through `obs`. `S` itself reads only
   monotone inputs from `T`. The quotient replaces `S` by the blocks of a partition of an
   extended system `X` that is stable for divergence-blind stuttering bisimulation. We prove that
   the reduced cone is a divergence-blind stuttering bisimulation of the cone (Theorem 2), and
   that, with a *can loop* mark on every block with a silent cycle, a fair-cycle search on the
   reduced cone is sound for `WF(Next)` (Theorem 3).
2. *A state-dependent symmetry group* (Section 5). `G_s` permutes only the slots that are not
   absent in `s` and relabels foreign keys. Under five syntactic conditions and the semantics of
   delete (G1–G6), the orbit relation is
   a bisimulation on the states whose absent rows hold base values (Theorem 4). Quotient edges
   carry permutations, fair instances move along them, and the fair-cycle search on the annotated
   quotient decides `eventually` and `leads_to` exactly (Theorem 5).
3. *Certificates and fallback* (Section 6). The proofs need facts about data the tool computes:
   the split, the partition, the loop marks, the rise table, the symmetry hypotheses. Every run
   checks these facts. A failed split, loop-mark, rise or binding check makes the tool decide the
   cone unreduced. A partition that fails its check is replaced by a fallback partition. A class
   that fails the symmetry scan is left out of the group. Lean proves sound a model of the
   partition check and two checkers that scripts run on the dumped splits and flat partitions.
   Every printed counterexample is lifted to the unreduced cone and replayed on the built system.
4. *Evaluation* (Section 7). An ablation of each reduction on nine models, scaling in the number
   of instances, determinism across thread counts, the smallest instance count at which each of
   132 known counterexamples appears, and verdict agreement with TLC.

The theorems are stated over LC, a calculus of entities, slots, owners and guarded actions
(Section 3). The flat cases of Theorems 2–5 and the fair-cycle search are mechanized in Lean 4
(about 14k lines, no Mathlib, no `sorry`; Appendix A). Two steps are argued on paper: the cases of
(G6) outside the Lean model's guard form, and the invariance of the tool's property forms under
`G_s` (Theorem 5 takes it as a hypothesis). The link from the Rust implementation to
LC is validated (Section 6), not proved. We say so wherever it matters.

# 2 Two examples

Both examples are cases of the tool's engine corpus. CI checks each one with the native checker
and with TLC on the unreduced TLA+ module.

**A loop that a quotient hides.** Three entities: a key, a gate owned by the key, and jobs owned
by the gate.

```
entity Key  { status: Enum(locked, unlocked) }
entity Gate { status: Enum(shut, open)  latch: Bool }
entity Job  { status: Enum(waiting, running) }
relate(Gate, Key, N:1)   relate(Job, Gate, N:1)
Unlock:  Key.status  locked -> unlocked
Unlatch: Gate.latch := true    when latch == false
Relatch: Gate.latch := false   when latch == true
Swing:   Gate.status shut -> open  when latch == true and Key.status == unlocked
Submit:  creates Job
Start:   Job.status waiting -> running   when Gate.status == open
property JobStartsUnfair  eventually(Job.status == running)
```

The property is checked under `WF(Next)`. On the cone, the behaviour that submits a job and then
runs `Unlatch`, `Relatch`, `Unlatch`, … forever never unlocks the key. It changes the state
infinitely often, so it satisfies `WF(Next)`, and the job never starts: a counterexample. The
gate is an independent sub-lifecycle of the job's cone: the job reads only `Gate.status == open`.
The sub-lifecycle holds the gate and its key. A stable partition groups its states by what the
job observes, so the states of a shut gate, whatever its latch and its key, fall into one block.
In the quotient that block has a silent move towards the open gate, and no loop. A `WF(Next)`
search on the quotient alone therefore never stops in it, and answers `ok`.
Theorem 3 fixes this with the *can loop* mark: a block whose silent edges between different
states contain a cycle may be a place where the quotient's behaviour stops. With the mark, the
search reports the lasso. The tool lifts it to a one-step prefix (`Submit`) and the two-step
latch loop (`Unlatch`, `Relatch`), and replays it on the cone. The reduced cone has 54 states, and the
unreduced cone (1,784 states) gives the same verdict.

**Slots that are not interchangeable.**

```
entity Order { status: Enum(open, done)  rush: Bool }
entity Gate  { state: Enum(closed, open) }
Build:     creates Gate
Place:     creates Order
PlaceRush: creates Order with rush := true   when Gate.state == open
Finish:    Order.status open -> done          when rush == false
OpenGate:  Gate.state closed -> open          when some Order is done
property RushFinishes  eventually(Order.status == done).fair(Finish)
```

The gate opens only after some order is done, and a rush order needs the open gate. So the first
order is never a rush order. Creation takes the lowest absent slot, so the first order is always
`Order#1`, and only `Order#2` can be a rush order, which never finishes. At two instances the
property fails, and only for `Order#2`. The system has 41 reachable states. In each one in which
only `Order#1` exists, swapping the two order slots gives a state in which `Order#2` exists
and `Order#1` is absent, and no run reaches it. A group that moves absent slots is therefore no
symmetry of the system. `G_s` fixes absent slots, so in a state with one order it is trivial, and
in a state with two orders it may swap them. The representative of an orbit need not be
reachable: the swap of a state with `Order#1` done and a rush `Order#2` is unreachable but in the
same `G_s`-orbit, which Theorem 4 allows. A run up to `G_s` (forced on this small cone, as the
corpus does) finds the counterexample at `Order#2` and lifts it.

# 3 The calculus LC

LC keeps from rdra-ish what the reductions need: entities with bounded slots, finite-domain
columns, owners (foreign keys), and guarded actions with per-instance weak fairness. The full
definitions are in the technical report. Most constructs that LC omits (raised events, split use
cases, the clock) map to LC columns and guards. Copies between columns, cones that leave an event
mark out, guard folds and nested reductions need arguments of their own, which the technical
report gives.

**Signature and states.** A signature has entities `E`, columns `c ∈ Col(E)` with finite domains
`D_c` and base values, and owners `o ∈ Own(E)` with a parent entity `par(o)`, each mandatory or
optional. A bound `n` gives each entity `n_E` slots. A state `s` assigns to each slot `i` of `E` an
existence `ex_E(s, i) ∈ {absent, present, deleted}`, to each column a value of `D_c`, and to each
owner a parent slot or `⊥`.

**Actions.** An action has parameters, each with a role: `new` (the lowest absent slot), `ex` (any
present slot) or `par(o, q)` (the present parent of the row `q` along `o`). It also has a guard
over the parameters and an effect. Guards are Boolean combinations of `=`, `≠` and membership on
columns, owners and slot ids, with quantifiers over present slots. Effects create, delete, assign
literals, choose from a set, follow a transition table, and link or unlink owners. All updates
apply to the pre-state at once, and choices fork the successor. A delete resets the row's columns
to base values and keeps its owners. A slot never returns to `absent`.

**Semantics.** `T_n(M) = (S, I, →)` is finite. A *behaviour* is an infinite sequence of states
in which each step is a transition or a stutter. A *fair instance* of a property `P` is a pair
`(a, κ)` of a fair action and the slots of its non-`new` parameters. The instance is enabled at
`s` when some admissible binding extending `κ` leads to another state, and a step to another
state takes it when some such binding produces the step. `Fair_P` is weak fairness for every
instance. `WfNext` is `Fair_P ∧ WF(Next)`. `leads_to(x, y)` holds when, for every behaviour
satisfying the fairness condition and every binding `b` of its rows, every position where the
rows are present and `x_b` holds is followed by one where `y_b` holds. `eventually(y)` is
`leads_to(true, y)`.

**Cones.** For an action `a`, `Rd(a)` and `Wr(a)` are its syntactic read and write sets. For a
seed `σ` (the variables a check reads), the cone `W` is the least set that contains `σ` and the
existences of its entities and parents, and that contains `Rd(a)` for every action writing `W`.
`T|W` projects onto `W`. When no action of the cone creates a row whose existence is outside `W`,
`Reach(T|W) = π_W(Reach(T))`, and traces lift step by step (Theorem 1). The reductions below
apply to cone systems.

# 4 The sub-lifecycle quotient

Let `C` be a cone system over `V_C`, and `seeds` the variables its checks read.

**Split.** A split partitions `V_C = T_v ⊎ S_v` and the writing actions into `T_a ⊎ S_a`. Define
`obs = (⋃_{a ∈ T_a} Rd(a) ∪ seeds) ∩ S_v`, and let `inputs ⊆ T_v` be the `T_v` variables that `S_a`
actions read, together with their existences. A variable is *monotone* when the pairs of values its
effects write form an acyclic graph apart from self-loops. The split is *admissible* when:

- (S1) every `S_a` action writes only `S_v`;
- (S2) every `T_a` action writes only `T_v` and reads `S_v` only in `obs`;
- (S3) every `S_a` action reads only `S_v ∪ inputs`;
- (S4) every input is monotone;
- (S5) the seeds read `S_v` only in `obs`, and every fair or kept action is in `T_a`.

The tool computes the least admissible split that keeps a chosen anchor variable in `T_v`, over
all anchors, and keeps the one with the fewest `T` variables. The theorems need only
admissibility, which every run checks (Section 6).

**The extended system and the quotient.** `X` is the system over `S_v ∪ inputs` whose *silent*
edges are the `S_a` steps and whose *rises* are the restrictions of the `T_a` actions that write
an input. A silent edge keeps the inputs, and a rise keeps `S_v`. A state `(t, s)` of `C` corresponds
to `x(t, s) = (s, t|_inputs)` in `X`. Monotone inputs bound how often a rise can happen, which keeps
the reachable part of `X` small. A partition `B` of `Reach(X)` is *stable* when:

- (B1) the states of a block agree on `obs` and the inputs;
- (B2) for blocks `B ≠ B'`, either every state of `B` reaches `B'` by silent edges through `B`, or
  none does (divergence-blind stuttering stability);
- (B3) the states of a block reach the same blocks by one rise.

The *reduced cone* `R` has states `(t, ρ)`, where `ρ` is a representative of a block. It runs the
`T_a` actions unchanged, moves the block along the matching rises when a `T_a` step changes the
inputs, and replaces the `S_a` actions by *class moves*, from a block to each block its
representative enters by (B2).

**Theorem 2 (reduction).** *If the split is admissible, `B` is stable, and each representative is
a reachable state of its block, then `(t, s) ~ (t, ρ(x(t, s)))` is a divergence-blind stuttering
bisimulation between `C` and `R` that preserves `T_v`, `obs` and the label of every `T_a` step.
Hence `C` and `R` agree on reachable valuations of `T_v ∪ obs`, on which `T_a` labels occur, on
stuck states for changes of `T_v`, and on every liveness property over `T_v ∪ obs` with fair actions
in `T_a` under `Fair_P`.*

*Proof sketch.* Lemma 6 of the technical report relates `C` and `X`: an `S_a` step of `C` is a
silent edge of `X` and back, and a `T_a` step that changes the inputs is a rise. `T_a`
steps read `S_v` only through `obs`, which (B1) fixes on a block, so they match exactly. An `S_a`
step either stays in its block (a stutter of `R`) or enters a block `B'`, and (B2) gives a matching
class move from the representative. Conversely a class move lifts, by (B2) and Lemma 6, to a path
of `S_a` steps through states related to the source. (B3) carries the rises. For `Fair_P`
liveness, a behaviour of `C` that steps forever inside one block maps to a behaviour of `R` that
stops there. All fair actions are `T_a` actions, whose enabledness is constant on the block, so
`Fair_P` allows the stop exactly when it allows the `C` behaviour. ∎

Divergence blindness is what makes the quotient small: a block may contain silent cycles. It is
also why Theorem 2 stops at `Fair_P`. Under `WF(Next)`, the `C` behaviour that cycles inside a block
is allowed, while its image in `R`, which stops in a block with a move out, is not. Section 2's
first example is this case.

**Definition (can loop).** A block *can loop* when the silent edges between distinct states of the
block contain a cycle. The search on `R` uses the stop rule "no fair instance is enabled, and either
no move leaves the state or its block can loop".

**Theorem 3 (`WF(Next)` on the reduced cone).** *Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2, if `X` and `R`
are finite, every block with a silent cycle is marked, and the fair-cycle search on the complete
graph of `R` with the looping stop rule finds no violation, then `C` satisfies the property under
`WfNext`.*

*Proof sketch.* Take a violating behaviour `σ` of `C` under `WfNext` and its image in `R`. If the
image changes infinitely often, it eventually stays in a strongly connected set of goal-free states
in which every fair instance is either taken or disabled somewhere, a fair component that the
search finds. If the image is eventually constant but `σ` is not, `σ` steps forever inside one
block. The enabledness of its fair instances is then constant (they are `T_a` actions, and `T_v`
and `obs` are constant), and one that is enabled would be taken under `Fair_P` and change `T_v`,
so all are disabled. The
state-changing steps of `σ` form an infinite walk in the block's finite silent graph, so the
graph has a cycle, the block is marked, and the image may stop there. If `σ` stutters forever at
`(t, s)`, `WF(Next)` says no step leaves `(t, s)`. A move of `R` from the image would lift to one
that leaves `(t, s)`, so the image has none and may stop. ∎

The mark is not required to be tight: an extra mark adds stops and so only adds candidate
counterexamples. Each candidate is lifted to `C` and replayed. A candidate that does not replay, or
that fails `WF(Next)` on `C`, makes the tool decide the property on the unreduced cone.

**Nesting.** `X` may itself be reduced by an inner split. The tool does so, and the Lean
development has the composition of inner maps (`Nested.lean`). The step that relates the code's
outer representative to a reachable state of `X` is a paper argument. The paper's theorems cover
the flat case.

# 5 Instance symmetry with a state-dependent group

**The group.** The *classes* are the entities, except that a child whose slot `i` is owned by the
parent's slot `i` is coupled to its parent. Some classes are *fixed*: they are left out of the
group because the model breaks one of the hypotheses below for them. For a state `s`, `G_s` is the
product, over the classes that are not fixed, of the permutations of the slots that are not absent
in `s`. `g ∈ G_s` moves each slot's columns and existence with the slot, and relabels each owner on a
row that is not absent by the parent class's permutation. An owner on an absent row keeps its
value.

**Hypotheses.** For each class that moves:

- (G1) a term naming a row of the class is compared only by `=` or `≠` with a term naming a row of
  the same class (or an optional owner with `⊥`), and appears in no set membership;
- (G2) an owner is written only from a parameter of the parent class or `⊥`, a choice over an owner
  ranges over every row, and no column is written from a slot id;
- (G3) the initial states are closed under `G_s`: no start pins a slot;
- (G4) a creation writes every mandatory owner of the created row;
- (G5) a delete keeps the owners of the deleted row, and the group relabels them;
- (G6) no guard reads the owner of the row being created, before the step, when that owner's parent
  class moves.

Theorem 4 also uses two facts of the builder, which the scan does not check: every `new` parameter
is created by its action, and a parameter bound by a split use case's run mark carries the mark's
guard. The proof also reads the model as well-typed: a term reads, and an effect writes, a column
of its own entity.

The tool checks (G1)–(G4) and (G6) syntactically and fixes every class that fails one. (G5) is part
of the semantics. Fixing more classes only shrinks the group (`Sym.sub` in Lean), so leaving out a
class costs reduction, never soundness.

**Theorem 4 (symmetry).** *Let `W` be the set of states in which every absent slot holds base
values. `W` contains the initial states and is closed under steps and under every `G_s`. Under
(G1)–(G6), for every `s ∈ W`, `g ∈ G_s` and step `s →_{a,b} t`, `g(s) →_{a,g(b)} g(t)` is a step and
`g ∈ G_t`. Hence `{(s, g(s)) : g ∈ G_s}` is an equivalence and a bisimulation on `W`. A
breadth-first search that stores one member of each orbit, chosen as any function of the state,
reaches exactly the orbits of the reachable states.*

The proof goes step by step. The lowest absent slot is fixed by `g`. Present and deleted slots map
to slots of the same existence. Guards are invariant by (G1), (G6) and the restriction to `W`.
Effects commute by (G2), (G4) and (G5). The restriction to `W` is necessary. The Lean development
has a state outside `W` where an absent row's optional owner names a row, and creation without
writing that owner breaks commutation. It also has a system whose steps commute on the reachable
states only, where the quotient stores an unreachable image and an invariant changes verdict. So
commutation on reachable states is not enough.

**Liveness on the quotient.** Fair liveness reads *which* instance is taken along a path, so the
plain quotient graph does not suffice. Each stored edge keeps the permutation `σ` that took the
concrete successor to the stored one. A binding, and each fair instance, moves along a path by
these permutations: its *thread*. The search runs on pairs (stored state, binding). A component of
pairs is fair when it has a cycle and every thread, followed around it, is taken on one of its edges
or is disabled at one of its pairs. An
*orbit self-edge* (a step whose successor is a different state in the same orbit) counts as a move.

**Theorem 5 (liveness up to symmetry).** *Assume Theorem 4's commutation on `W`, that the group acts
on fair instances compatibly with action labels, that the trigger and the stop rule are invariant
under `G_s`, that the goal is invariant after a trigger, that the canonical form is a member of the
orbit chosen as a function of the state, and that the reachable states and every `G_s` are finite.
Then a property holds for every binding under `Fair_P` (or `WfNext`) iff no annotated lasso of the
canonical quotient that is thread-fair and stops only where the stop rule allows violates it for
any binding. A violating annotated lasso lifts to a violating lasso of `T` by unrolling its
cycle `d` times, where `d` is the order of the cycle's composite permutation.*

The invariance of the trigger and the goal is argued on paper for the tool's property forms. The
Lean model has no property syntax and takes it as a hypothesis. This is the Emerson–Sistla
annotated quotient for a state-dependent group, with weak fairness per
action instance. The group's dependence on the state matters in two places: frames compose only
because `G_{g(s)} = G_s` and steps keep `g ∈ G_t`, and the lifted lasso closes only after `d` copies.
Liveness runs up to `G_s` are used for `eventually` and `leads_to`. The tool runs `stable`,
`unchanged`, `precedes` and `can_reach` without symmetry.

# 6 Certificates and fallback

The theorems have hypotheses about data the implementation computes, and the implementation is
Rust, not LC. We do not prove the implementation correct. Instead, every run checks the hypotheses
that depend on computed data, and the tool replays its own counterexamples. The table gives each
check and what a failure does. The run-time checks are Rust code. Lean proves sound a model of the
partition check (`Sweep.stable_of_cert`), and two separate checkers (`Lemma5Check.check_sound`,
`PCert.stable_of_check`) that scripts run on the dumped top-level splits and flat partitions of the
samples and the corpus. The checks are literal on a flat `X`. For a nested `X`, or one run up to a
subgroup, they read the stored graph, and part of the argument stays on paper. When the
sub-lifecycle has no observed variable, `X` is not explored in a release build, and its loop
condition is checked only in test builds.

| Hypothesis | Run-time check | On failure | Lean counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Split admissible (S1–S5) | recompute `Rd`, `Wr` and monotonicity from the actions and check each condition on the split | cone decided unreduced, warning | `Lemma5Check.check_sound` |
| Partition stable (B1–B3) | sweep the explored `X`: block-local silent reachability and rise targets | fall back to the sweeps' partition, warning | `Sweep.stable_of_cert` (model of the check); `PCert.stable_of_check` (offline checker) |
| *can loop* covers every silent cycle | peel the silent edges inside unmarked blocks; a remaining state lies on a cycle | cone decided unreduced | — |
| Rise table, layout of `X` | each rise of a representative is one stage; inputs laid out as `R` reads them | cone decided unreduced | — |
| Bindings in `X` | no parameter of `X` that needs a present row sits on an entity `X` does not hold | cone decided unreduced | — |
| (G1)–(G4), (G6) | syntactic scan of every guard and effect | class fixed (left out of `G_s`) | — |
| Any counterexample | lift to the unreduced cone through class moves and permutations; replay on the built system's step relation; check the fairness of a lasso | `tool_error` (never printed as a counterexample); a reduced liveness candidate that fails goes to the unreduced cone | — |

Replay is against the system the tool built, so it cannot catch a fault in the builder itself.
The step from the cone to the whole model rests on Theorem 1. A counterexample of a run up to
symmetry on a reduced cone is never printed: plain runs decide that cone again.

What remains read from the code rather than checked: that the code's actions are LC syntax (the
Lean slot model shares its syntax), eight facts about which guards the restriction to `X` keeps and
about the guard folds (an optimization of `X` that LC does not have), and that the canonical form,
Tarjan's components and the bit sets of the search implement their Lean counterparts. Section 8
lists these as threats. The defect record of the project supports the design (Section 7.6): the
defect that broke the `C ⇒ X` half of the reduction's simulation (a parameter on a stub entity
that never bound in `X`) printed a wrong `ok`. The binding check above was added for it, and now
catches that class of defect on every run.

# 7 Evaluation

All numbers come from `scripts/paper/eval.py` on main at `9e4a123`. The machine is a 4-core Linux
x86_64 VM with 15 GiB of memory. The build is `cargo build --release --features perf`, and the runs
use `RDRA_JOBS=1`. CPU times are user CPU of the process, and memory is its peak RSS. The data and

## 7.3 How many instances find the known counterexamples? (RQ3)

Across the 91 corpus cases and the failing sample headers, 132 expected counterexamples are
known. 106 of them appear at one instance per entity, and 24 first appear at two. The remaining
two need per-entity counts above the uniform bound of three (`--instances Loan=4`) or a memory cap
that the header sets. No counterexample needs three uniform instances where two do not suffice.
Most two-instance cases are the ones the corpus wrote to need two rows: uniqueness, foreign-key
moves, and the asymmetry of Section 2. This supports the tool's default of two instances for
`verify` and the use of `--prove` for statements about any number of rows. It is evidence about
these models, not a small-scope theorem ([eval/minimal.md](./eval/minimal.md)).

## 7.6 What found the defects? (RQ6)

The project's history records 74 defects in the verification pipeline
([evidence/defects.md](./evidence/defects.md)). 30 of them printed a wrong verdict on some model. 19
were found while writing or auditing the theory documents (including the `--prove` mode, outside
this paper), 13 of them wrong verdicts,
and 6 of those a wrong `ok` or `proved` that the native checker and TLC agreed on or that no
comparison reached. Differential
testing against TLC and the engine corpus found 7. The Lean proofs, the reference interpreter, the
run-time certificates and the reference twins are recent (November 2026), and no defect on record
was first found by them: the history shows them catching defects that were already known or
planted. We draw a narrow conclusion. Agreement between engines does not see common-mode defects in
the shared builder, the cone choice or the quotient tables. Stating each guarantee as a theorem
with named hypotheses found those defects, and the certificates now check the hypotheses on every
run. Paper 2 studies this in detail.

# 8 Related work and limitations

[related-work.md](./related-work.md) has the comparison table. In brief:

- **Symmetry reduction.** Ip and Dill's scalarsets and Emerson and Sistla's annotated quotients
  use a fixed group of process indices. Emerson and Sistla handle fairness through annotations,
  which Theorem 5 adapts to a group that depends on the state. Virtual symmetry and guarded
  annotated quotients relax global symmetry for asymmetric systems. dSPIN canonicalizes heap
  objects per state, allocated so far, and relabels references. That is the nearest prior work for
  allocation with relabelled references, but it has no fairness transfer or mechanized proof.
  TLC's symmetry sets permute model values and are documented as unsound for liveness. ProB permutes
  deferred sets. Alloy breaks symmetry statically over atoms.
- **Stuttering quotients.** Divergence-blind and divergence-sensitive stuttering bisimulation
  (Browne, Clarke and Grumberg; De Nicola and Vaandrager; Groote and Vaandrager's algorithm)
  preserve CTL\* without next, the divergence-sensitive variant also under divergence. Theorem 3
  keeps the coarser divergence-blind partition and adds back the stops it hides, with one mark per
  block.
- **Data-centric processes.** Artifact-centric verification (Deutsch, Hull, Vianu; Calvanese,
  Montali; Belardinelli and Lomuscio) and VERIFAS decide properties of unbounded data under
  restrictions. rdra-ish is bounded, and adds fair liveness with two composed reductions and lifted
  traces. It is a different point in the design space, not a generalization.
- **Parameterized verification.** Cutoff results transfer a result at a cutoff size to every size
  for fully symmetric processes. Ivy-style inference proves safety for unbounded sorts. rdra-ish's
  `--prove` mode is of that kind and is outside this paper.
- **Certifying model checkers.** Namjoshi's certifying model checkers emit proofs of `ok`.
  rdra-ish certifies the *hypotheses* of its reductions and its counterexamples, not its `ok`. A
  certificate for `ok` is future work (roadmap T15).

**Limitations and threats.**

- *(T1) Models.* The models were written by the tool's authors. Five are written from public
  normative texts, but none comes from an outside modeler (roadmap T9).
- *(T2) The code link.* The theorems are about LC. The implementation is checked against the
  hypotheses at run time and tested differentially, but eight facts of the restriction to `X`, the
  syntactic scan, the canonical form and the search's data structures are read from the code
  (Section 6). The nested reduction and the run of `S` up to a subgroup are argued on paper.
- *(T3) No run-time check of `ok`.* A counterexample is replayed, but an `ok` is not checked. A fault
  outside the checked hypotheses that gives a wrong `ok` passes unnoticed in the run.
- *(T4) Symmetry is experimental.* `--symmetry always` is not the default, and no scan rule fires
  on the samples, so the hypotheses' necessity is shown on unit witnesses only.
- *(T5) Bounded results.* Every verdict is at a bound. Section 7.3 is evidence about these models.

# 9 Conclusion

Fair liveness on lifecycle models with creation and foreign keys needs reductions that respect
three things: creation order, the foreign keys between rows, and weak fairness of the next-state
relation. We gave two such reductions, proved them over a small calculus, mechanized their flat
cases, and built an implementation that checks their hypotheses on every run and falls back
when a check fails. The sub-lifecycle quotient decides models that the unreduced checker does not
finish. Instance symmetry decides liveness exactly on a quotient whose group changes with the
state.
